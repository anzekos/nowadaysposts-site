There are two kinds of Pinterest data and they tell very different stories. Search trends are what people are dreaming about — right now that is porches, mums, chalkboards and doorways, all of it outdoors and all of it photogenic. The trending product categories are ranked by outbound clicks: what people actually left the app to go and buy. This month that list reads cookware +88%, seasonal decorations +84%, wreaths and garlands +56%, fireplace and wood stove accessories +55%, decorative bowls +28% and table runners +16%. Almost none of it is on the porch. The dreaming is still outside; the spending has already moved indoors, into the kitchen, the hearth, the hallway and the middle of the table. Here are five objects, one from each of those categories, chosen because they do a real job and still look like something.

**The kitchen, and the pot that stopped being equipment.** Cookware is the fastest-growing category on the list by outbound clicks, which is strange until you notice which cookware gets saved: never the nonstick set, always one heavy pot in a colour, photographed on the stove. That is not a cooking decision, it is a storage decision. A pot that lives in a cupboard has to be justified; a pot that lives out has to be looked at. This one is six quarts of enameled cast iron in a soft matte cream — 9.4 inches across, 5 deep, 12.9 lbs, with a thin black rim line, a domed lid with concentric rings and a pale butter-cream interior. The domed lid is the functional half: condensation collects on the inside and drips back down over whatever is braising, which is why the same pot handles Sunday soup and a sourdough loaf. Oven safe to 500F, fine on gas, electric, induction and ceramic. Twelve pounds is a lot to lift down from a shelf twice a week, which is the practical argument for leaving it where it looks best anyway. 4.6 stars from 537.

**The hearth, and the pile of wood that becomes part of the room.** Fireplace and wood stove accessories are up 55%, and the timing is not a mystery: this is the fortnight when the first fire of the year gets lit and everyone discovers their firewood is stacked on the floor on a sheet of newspaper. Firewood has to live somewhere in the room, so the only question is whether the somewhere is a container or a corner. This is a 25-quart oval tub in antique copper — hammered bands top and bottom, a smooth band across the middle, two iron handles hinged at the ends, low and wide rather than tall. The proportions are what make it work beside a hearth: wide enough that four or five split logs sit with their cut ends facing out the way you stacked them, low enough that it reads as furniture rather than a bin. It carries full and empties in one trip, and in April, when the fire is out for the year, it holds blankets, pinecones or nothing at all and still earns its place. 4.7 stars from 160.

**The hallway, and the pile that needed a shape.** Decorative bowls are up 28%, which is the quietest number on the list and probably the most useful. A decorative bowl is not decoration; it is a boundary around a mess that already exists. Keys, coins, a single earring and yesterday's receipt land on the console table whether or not you have made a plan for them, and the difference between clutter and a still life is a defined edge. This one is 7.8 inches across and only 2 inches deep, cut from natural travertine, so it is pitted and veined differently in every piece and heavy enough to stay exactly where it is when the front door slams. Stone is the right material here for an unglamorous reason: it does not scratch when keys are thrown into it and it does not slide. Wide and low means things land in it flat instead of piling, so the pile reads as arrangement. It works just as well on a coffee table with two limes in it, or empty. 4.7 stars from 186.

**The table, and the line that replaces the centerpiece.** Table runners are only up 16%, the smallest rise on the list, but they are the object that solves the season's most common table problem: a bare table needs one line down the middle, not a floristry project with a four-day shelf life. This is 13 by 90 inches of ivory cotton-linen with a chunky basketweave, fine taupe stripe rows and small tufted dots scattered in loose diagonal groups, finishing in thick hand-knotted tassels that hang over each end. The length matters more than the pattern — 90 inches covers a six-seater with a fall at both ends, which is what makes the table look set rather than dressed. It is washable, which is the whole argument for a woven runner over anything with a pile, and neutral enough to stay on the table from September through to the new year, with the candles and the leaves changing around it. 4.8 stars from 942.

**The doorway, and the seasonal decoration that does not need putting away in a week.** Seasonal decorations are up 84% and wreaths and garlands 56%, and with Halloween five weeks out those two categories are converging on exactly one shape: something dark over a door frame. The trap is that most of it is disposable — spiders, cobwebs and orange plastic that comes down on the 1st of November. Six feet of black roses, black eucalyptus and berry sprigs on a bendable vine does the same job and reads elegant first, Halloween second, so it stays up through November if you want it to. Ten full blooms spaced unevenly along the vine, dense enough to hide the wire in places and open enough to see the white frame through it elsewhere. Because the vine bends, it is not only a door piece: the same garland drapes a mantel, winds down a stair rail or lies flat down the centre of the table with candles between the roses. 4.5 stars from 299.

None of these five is on a porch, and that is the point. The pictures people save in late September are still outdoor pictures, but the purchases have already turned inward — a pot that stays on the stove, a tub by the fire, a bowl by the door, a line down the table and something dark over the doorway. The porch gets photographed. These are what the house is actually doing.
